Blocks and Stories

image to come

What you need

Wooden building blocks of a variety of shapes and sizes. Flat floor space within which to build as well as uninterrupted time to do so, including leaving the creations out on display, both complete and incomplete.

Images of buildings, eg castles, towers, bridges etc. NEED IMAGE

A vertical flat surface nearby to display photographs of creations and other construction images.

What to do

  • Play alongside children, either building your own construction or building collaboratively. Commentate what you are doing and why you are selecting a particular block, eg: I think this sloping shape might make a good roof.

  • Displaying both the constructions themselves and collecting photographs of the built and partially built structures values the block play and provides opportunities to develop what is taking place over time. For example, when appropriate you could introduce a story context with a problem to solve, e.g. a bridge for a lorry to make a delivery to the castle, or a house big enough for a toy elephant.

What to try next

  • Introduce a familiar story to the block area.
  • When appropriate, add clipboards and pens for children to draw their creations.
  • Take photographs of the building to make into a floor book, which will demonstrate developing spatial awareness and creativity over time.
  • Take construction outside by providing items to build with e.g. blocks, planks, tyres, crates, log-slices etc.

What to say

  • This block is too short to stretch across here. I need a longer one that’s just the same shape.
  • I need two blocks the same height to make my bridge.
  • I wonder if this block is flat enough to stand up by itself?
  • I wonder … what shall we build today?
  • Oooo, what shapes do we need to make an arch?

What might happen

  • Young children begin by enjoying knocking down towers built by adults, and progress to adding blocks to towers built collaboratively. Next, come lines of blocks to make ‘roads’ and gradually walls, bridges and enclosures. All are important stages of thinking and getting to know the properties of different shapes of block.

  • As children become more experienced, you will see them begin to select blocks for a specific purpose and reject others, e.g. blocks of the same shape to make a road. This is an indication that they are visualising what they want to create.

You also might recognise aspects of symmetry – or ‘balance’ - in what they create. Draw their attention to this.

What matters

  • The most important thing is time and space. Time is necessary for the children to get fully involved and gradually learn to collaborate with others creating structures and buildings they can play with.
  • Talking together with adults about the children’s creations after the play has finished gives children the distance to think and talk about what they have been involved in, and for the adult to introduce vocabulary to describe the structures. Time after the play provides reflection time for the children, and opportunities for them to discuss developments and improvements as well as for the adult to provide further stimulus.
  • Unit blocks are the ideal resource, i.e. those related to each other in size and shape, so that two of these make one of these (ADD IMAGE). Also providing a wide range of materials for construction indoors and outdoors is important, including a range of recycled materials which provide real life examples of shapes e.g. kitchen roll tubes, tissue boxes, party hats, tyres, etc.

What to discuss

  • How did your children surprise you?

  • Did you notice children selecting particular blocks, shapes or lengths for specific purpose they had in mind?

The Research

Did you know that…

… Children’s block play is related to later mathematical competence and particular interest in STEM careers?

NEED content specific to block play, not just spatial reasoning. Also the adult role.

Block play provides a playful context for exploring the physical world and developing children’s spatial awareness. Children experiment with structures and observe the outcomes of their building efforts. Through this process they learn about different shapes and how they are related to one another, weight and balance.1

Mathematics research is clear: Early mathematics must contain both high-quality free play and high-quality teaching.2,3,4,5
