Cutting a square

image to come

What you need

Paper squares of about 10cm side length in different colours, scissors.

What to do

  • Each child selects a paper square. Chat about their choice and ask them what they know about a square. Model and talk through folding your square in half, diagonally, from corner-to-corner.ADD DIAGRAM Ask: What do you see now?
  • Open your folded square out and re-fold it, several times. Children do the same with their squares and chat about what they are doing.
  • Everyone cuts their square in half along the diagonal fold to make two triangles. Place the two triangles on top of each other. Ask: Do they fit? Are they the same shape and size as each other? Can children re-make their square from their two triangles? What new shapes can they make by fitting their two triangles together? ADD DIAGRAM
  • Encourage children to describe what they make, using their own language, whilst you introduce vocabulary such as: similar, different, straight, corner, point, side, edge, touching, half/ halved.
  • Would children like to choose one of their new shapes to glue onto a poster? Leave out the poster and more paper squares for them to re-create the shapes displayed. Observe children playing with the materials. They may cut the squares into many different pieces!
  • Revisit the activity, this time folding and cutting the square in half to make two rectangles. Ask: What can we make with these?

What to try next

  • Try starting with a different shape and halving this, e.g. a rectangle or a triangle.
  • Children cut their square into 4 triangles by making a second diagonal fold (“sandwiches”). ADD DIAGRAM This second cut presents many more possibilities. Play with re-arranging the 4 triangles into new shapes and remaking the original square.
  • Explore tiling (covering / filling) a surface or region with many similar shapes, leaving no spaces; for example, all paper squares or triangles.
  • Introduce a set of pattern blocks for children to explore. These are related by side-length or angle.ADD DIAGRAM
  • Visualising what will happen: Sit with a group of children, demonstrate folding a sheet of paper in half, and hovering your scissors where you will make a single cut. ADD DIAGRAM What do they think the piece in your hand will look like when you make the cut and open the new shape out? When modelled clearly, many children enjoy playing with this idea when paper and scissors are left out.

What to say

  • What do you think you might be able to make?
  • How can you describe what you see / what you have made?
  • Trace your finger all around the edge of your new shape.
  • Can you repair / remake your square?
  • I wonder if you could make a shape similar to mine / like hers? Different to ..?
  • If you place that triangle there, what do you think your new shape will look like?

What might happen

  • Some children might need practice folding accurately in half.
  • They might not be able to resist cutting the square into smaller and smaller pieces!
  • Children might start by assigning a name to the shapes they make after it is made, like “Roof”, “Rocket”.
  • It takes time and experience for children to be able to predict what a new arrangement might look like, or to visualise and describe or name something they would like to make (prediction).
  • Keeping ‘their’ 4 triangles in a named envelope encourages children to revisit this task when they have an idea.

What matters

  • Spatial skills can be trained.1 Folding and cutting paper shapes gives children experience of how shapes are composed. Experience of constructing and de-constructing shapes, rather than simply recognising and naming already formed, usually regular, shapes is important. Re-arranging and putting shapes back together is a key way for young children to experience the conservation of area (measures), informally and playfully.
  • Spatial visualisation and prediction are key to developing children’s mathematical thinking. We can support this by wondering aloud what something might look like and asking questions like “What do you think it might look like if …” or “Can you imagine what it will look like if..?” Whilst children might not have the vocabulary to answer such questions, if we regularly ask these, we are drawing their attention to what matters; ie their thinking processes.
  • Using gesture is useful in helping children understand vocabulary such as “fold in half”, “under’, “on top” “in front” “beside”, “point”, etc.2
  • Recognition of what is similar and what is different in a variety of contexts is important mathematically.

Spatial reasoning involves how we understand the location and positions of objects (and us), and of the dimensions and properties of objects.
