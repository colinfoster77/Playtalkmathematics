The Three Bears

image to come

What you need

The picturebook of The Three Bears and a collection of different lengths of rod - Cuisenaire rods are ideal (image & reference here?).

What to do

Read and enjoy the story with the children. Before the second reading, invite them to each choose one rod to represent Daddy bear, one rod for Mummy bear and one for Baby bear. Allow them sufficient time to chat, choose, and to change their minds if they wish.
Now re-tell the story (slowly!) and when the children hear “Daddy Bear” they each hold up their Daddy Bear rods, on hearing “Mummy Bear” they hold up their Mummy Bear rods, and likewise for Baby Bear.

What to try next

  • Children hide their three Bears behind their backs to identify by feel alone.
  • Children lay out their rods/Bears lining up the end points carefully to look at different and similar choices and invite children to explain their decisions.They say what they notice about the different rods chosen and the differing lengths of rod.
  • Add challenge by reducing the range of rods to choose from, eg, the first five, as this would lead to finer comparisons being made, especially by feel alone.

OTHERS FROM JUNE VERSION? * Leave out the rods and the picture book for children to recreate the activity, telling the story themselves. * Invite children to draw long, short and middle-sized rods, sticks , people or bears. Encourage the use of a baseline to line up the endpoints. * Outdoors ask children to find a tall, middle sized and short stick. * Can they order a larger stick collection from longest to shortest? * Introduce a set of nesting cups or toys for children to explore and organise by size.

What to say

  • Can you tell us about your bear family - which bear is which? Can you describe how long each rod is?
  • Let’s line up your bears carefully to see how much taller/longer Daddy is than Baby.
  • Do you see anyone with the same rods as you have chosen? How can you be sure (prove) these are same length as each other?
  • Which is your middle-sized rod/bear? Can you show me?
  • How do you know which rod is which without looking?

OTHERS FROM JUNE VERSION? * That’s a different Mummy bear rod, isn’t it? I wonder why? * This is longer than/ taller than, this one, and this one is shorter than these….

What might happen

  • Children might spot others who have chosen the same rods as them, as well as comment when a different rod is held up to their own. Encourage them to explain why they made their choice, and other choices they might have made.
  • Children might need encouragement to align the ends of their rods.
  • When selecting rods by feel alone, children might check visually before holding up the rod.
  • They might want to choose a rod for Goldilocks. This will lead to interesting discussions about what they choose and how the length of this rod relates to their others. Remember it was Baby Bear’s bowl/chair/bed that were the correct size!

What matters

  • Comparisons are critical to learning about length, as well as to mathematics more generally. Something is only ‘long’ when compared to something that is shorter, ‘small’ in relation to something bigger.
  • Children benefit from a wide variety of experiences comparing the length of objects and learning to line up the end points to make accurate comparisons. The latter can be emphasised in this activity.
  • Discussions are important about who has chosen which rod, and why, to stand for each bear, as well as spotting similarities and differences between choices.

OTHERS FROM JUNE VERSION? * More than three lengths of rod are needed for children to choose from, in order they make decisions about what they see as long, short and middle sized. * Opportunities for children to make length comparisons and to develop their understanding of the language of measure, from big and small to using comparative language such as: middle sized, long (er than), tall (er than), short (er than), about as long as…, much shorter than…, (about) the same length as… etc.

What to discuss

  • What other opportunities do we have for children to compare lengths (and widths)?
  • What are we drawing our children’s attention to? How do we emphasize length as a distance between endpoints rather than as ‘one end sticking out’?
The Research

Did you know that…

…Studies suggest that early measuring experiences using different non-standard units (such as hand spans, conkers, etc) are unhelpful, as switching units frequently might send the message that the unit of measure doesn’t matter?

FROM JUNE VERSION Children as young as 2 become aware of size and begin to develop ideas of seriation.1,2 Without concrete experiences and reference, terms like ‘big’ and ‘small’ are difficult for young children to understand.3^
