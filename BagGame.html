The Bag Game

image to come

Possible icons to use - let’s agree what we like and then I can insert them

  • Play: Engage with interactive elements.
  • Talk: Discuss and share insights.
  • Manipulate: Adjust variables and experiment.
  • Adapt: Modify and evolve the concept.

What you need

An opaque (cloth) bag, a tray of pinecones or other similar objects.

What to do

The children sit in a ring. Show them the empty bag. The bag is passed around the ring with each child adding one pinecone in turn. They count along as each pinecone is added. Before reaching 5 cones in the bag, stop the count, and ask if we know how many cones are in the bag altogether. You may wish to ask how we know. After discussion, tip them out to check. Repeat as many times as they are engaged, starting each time with the empty bag, and when confident, add more than 5 before tipping the bag out to check.

What to try

  • Leave the bag and cones out for the children to re-create the game. Observe them playing with the materials. They may fill the bag with all or as many of the cones as possible.
  • Revisit the game starting with 6 cones into the bag together. Pass the bag of 6 cones around the ring, with each child removing one cone, counting backwards until the bag is empty.
  • When the game is known well, this structure can be used to count on. Stop the count at 3, agree there are 3 in the bag without looking, and saying “threeee, slowly add one more, “fourrrr”, pass the bag, “fiveeeee …” etc. Ask how many are in the bag and how we know before tipping out to check as before.
  • Introduce some recorded music to pass the bag to. Leave the pinecones and bag out. When the music stops, one cone is added to the bag.
  • Two similar boxes with lids. Count 3 bricks into each box. Move one brick from the first box into the second box. How many are in each box now? Reverse. Repeat.

What to say

  • How many are there altogether in the bag? Can you show me using your fingers?
  • How many are in the bag now? How do we know?
  • Fin said “Three”, so what does Salim say now?
  • Let’s count back as we take them out, together.
  • How many do you think are in the bag now?
  • Are you sure?
  • Let’s check!

What might happen

  • Children may choose to demonstrate the amount in the bag using their fingers rather than, or as well as, saying the amount. Both finger numbers and the spoken amount are to be encouraged.
  • Children might be confident adding one at a time up to 5, but not proficient counting backwards. Work on an amount up to and back from 3, then 4.
  • Children might know the count sequence backwards (or forwards) as a list of counting words, but not attach these words to the actual items.
  • At first children might not answer your question “How do we know?”, but keep asking this, and leave time for them to think about it.
  • A child might add 2 cones instead of 1. Treat this as a maths joke! “Ha! TWO more- does anyone know how many are in there now?!”

What matters

  • The items can be changed but must be similar in both type and colour, to aid counting of the whole group.
  • Starting (or ending) with an empty bag is important for experience of the ‘empty set’.
  • Understanding the cardinality principle involves knowing that the last number you say establishes the quantity of the whole group. Asking “How many are in the bag now?”, and “How many are there altogether in the bag?”, before tipping out the bag to check, draws children’s attention to the last number said.
  • Counting back is as important as counting forwards, and children will become more confident with counting back as they become familiar with how the game works.
The Research

Did you know that…

… understanding the directional property of the counting list is a core insight in early number learning?

What is the directional property of the counting list?

Children need to grasp that each number in the counting list represents a quantity that is one more than the one before, and one less than the one after. The “bag game” is useful because it focuses directly on this idea. Children add or remove objects from a bag and have to work out how the total changes.

More…

Unlike many counting activities, where children count objects they can see, this task does not allow them to rely only on pointing and saying number words. In some counting tasks, children can succeed by following a routine of matching each object to a number word without fully understanding how counting works.1,2 In contrast, the bag game requires them to think about how the quantity changes when one item is added or taken away.

The activity can be easily adapted to suit different levels. When small numbers are used (up to three or four), children may be able to keep track of the objects mentally without counting. However, with larger numbers, this is no longer possible. At that point, children need to use their knowledge of counting to work out the new total, moving forwards or backwards in the counting list.3,4

Research shows that even children who can count may not yet understand how numbers change when one is added or removed.5,6 This activity helps to reveal and develop that understanding. Overall, the bag game supports children in moving beyond simply reciting numbers, helping them understand how numbers are ordered and connected.7 This is an important foundation for later arithmetic.
