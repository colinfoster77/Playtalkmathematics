Little Shop

image to come

What you need

Real 1p coins, purses and wallets.A tray of items to buy, such as small cracker toys.

What to do

With the children, count up to 8 pennies into each purse or wallet and label the tray of toys “1p shop” or “Penny Stretchers”. Children are invited to choose items to buy from the shop and to check how much is (left) in their purse or wallet by tipping out their coins and counting them back into the purse/wallet, closing the purse and then saying aloud how much they have left to spend.

What to try next

  • You might choose to begin with 5 pennies to spend each.
  • One child could be the shopkeeper and keep count of the shop income!
  • Leave out the tray of items, purses and coins for more shopping. Introduce pens and clipboards to encourage list-writing.
  • This activity will also work as a Poundshop using £1 coins.
  • Play a game with 5 pennies or £1 coins, leaving some visible whilst hiding the rest in a purse for children to work out how many are hidden each time. ADD diagram? Ask children how they are working it out.

What to say

  • What will you choose to buy, first? How much do you have in your purse altogether to spend?
  • How much do you have to spend?
  • So, how many pennies/much money is left in your purse, now?
  • How many things have you bought so far?
  • Do you know how much is left in your purse without counting? How do you know that?
  • Could you afford to buy any more / two more things?

What might happen

  • Children might well need to continually recount what is left in their purse. Try asking if they need to count, or if they know without counting.
  • As the amount of money in the purse decreases, some children might be able to answer: “How much is left in your purse now?” without counting. This is evidence of understanding of the successor/predecessor function embedded in the count list i.e. knowing which number precedes (and succeeds) another.
  • Children might use the amount of items they have bought to work out how much money they have spent and then how much they have left to spend.

What matters

  • It is important that children count the pennies back into the purse and are then asked when the purse/wallet is closed: “how much/many do you have (left) to spend? Saying aloud how much they have left to spend once the coins are returned to the purse helps children name the whole quantity (cardinality) when this is unseen.
  • Asking “Do you know how much is left in your purse now?” encourages children to arithmetically reason, without counting.

What to discuss

What did you notice about the children’s arithmetic confidence?

The Research

Did you know that…

…This activity can support the emergence of arithmetic reasoning with concrete quantities? When children reason about how many must be hidden to yield the original total, they are effectively solving a subtraction problem of the form: starting numerosity − visible numerosity = hidden numerosity.

More…

Learning the counting principles and, crucially, understanding the successor/predecessor function embedded in the count list.1 It has been highlighted that successor/predecessor function understanding is tightly linked to children’s ability to coordinate symbolic knowledge (number words) with underlying quantity representations.2

Successful performance reflects not only counting proficiency but also an understanding of how addition and subtraction are inverse operations.3,4 Additionally, when the starting numerosity is fixed at 10, the activity can train number bonds and decomposition-based reasoning to 10.5 This activity is valuable because it can be flexibly adapted to children’s developmental level. With very small sets, it supports early exact quantity representations while, with larger sets, it places increasing demands on counting and cardinal understanding, thereby encouraging the use of counting as a problem-solving tool rather than as a rote procedure.6 It also fosters arithmetic reasoning focusing on the inverse relation between addition and subtraction and potentially promotes number bonds up to 10.

Children are more accurate counting into a container rather than simply moving or touching a row of items, or, importantly, using physical manipulatives rather than abstract/digital objects that cannot be moved or touched.7
