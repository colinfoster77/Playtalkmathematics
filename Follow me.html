Follow Me

image to come

What you need

Assorted loose parts and shapes, a small mat or card sheet for yourself and each child.

What to do

You model selecting one loose part at a time placing it on your mat to create a simple symmetrical design or picture. Children watch and “follow me” copying the design, piece by piece. Narrate what you are choosing and where you decide to place each piece e.g. I am going to put one of these shiny beads in each corner, and this pointy shape will be the top of the rocket …

Children can take turns being the leader – this will work best in pairs sitting alongside each other.

What to try next

  • Use a cloth to cover your mat and your finished design. Allowing children only glimpses, can they make one exactly the same as yours? Together, enjoy the big reveal when they think they have completed the task. Discuss the similarities and differences.
  • Try playing “Follow me” with Duplo, large blocks outside or threading beads.
  • As they become more experienced, you could introduce a small screen between the players, so the follower is listening to the instructions, and only seeing occasional peeps of the design.
  • Introduce some recorded music to pass the bag to. Leave the pinecones and bag out. When the music stops, one cone is added to the bag.
  • Try “Follow me” to find some hidden ‘treasure’: One child hides a toy and lays a paper trail for a friend to find it. As they follow the trail, encourage the child to describe where they are going: Straight on, around the chair, it’s under the rug!

What to say

  • I am going to make a car. I have found round shapes for wheels but first I am going to put this long rectangle in the middle for the chassis.
  • Can you follow what I am doing, one piece at a time?
  • Maybe you could try turning that around to see if it matches this one.
  • Where will you put that piece, I wonder?
  • Are you ready for me to do the next part of my picture? Is yours the same so far?
  • Oooo – stop a minute and check.
  • What do you notice?
  • How are they the same/different?
  • Can you think of a picture for us all to make? Remember to tell us where to put each piece.

What might happen

  • Some children will be focussed on choosing and placing each piece and find it hard to say what they are doing as they do it. You could try narrating for them eg I can see you putting the triangle at the top. Encourage them to describe their picture after it is complete.
  • You will find it useful to use gestures to help children understand the positional vocabulary.
  • The leader might be so interested in what they are designing that they work too fast for the follower to keep up! Children might need encouragement to keep an eye on what the follower is doing, as well as selecting and placing their own pieces. You might need to stop them and invite them to take a careful check on what is going on.

What matters

  • This activity is focussed on recognising and describing spatial orientation and spotting similarities and differences in a shape, pattern or geometric structure.
  • Firstly, children learn to listen and respond appropriately to the instructional, descriptive and positional vocabulary used by the adult. Keep initial designs simple and make sure the resources used are familiar to the children i.e. they have previously used them freely and extensively.
  • It is more of a challenge for children to describe what they are doing with sufficient accuracy to create something the same. What is important is that they begin to recognise what they see as “the same” and “different”, and point this out, or correct it.
  • Be aware that words such as ‘on top’, ‘under’ ,‘beside’ could have several interpretations! This can lead to worthwhile discussions about what these words mean on different situations. NEED image
  • It is important to be as precise as possible when describing something (children are good word learners) but also to use the informal language which is familiar to children alongside more formal mathematical language.

What to discuss

  • How can we connect maths language to what children are currently doing and interested in?
  • What do we notice about the words the children use themselves when engaged in this activity?
