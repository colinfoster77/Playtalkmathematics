What’s missing?

image to come

What you need

A tray of up to 6 familiar items such as: toothbrush, pen, Duplo block, spoon, ball, play-person. A cloth to cover the tray. Decide on the amount and type of objects according to your knowledge of, and the ages of, the children.

What to do

  • Together name each item in turn, whilst placing them on the tray. Without using the cloth, remove and hide one item and ask children if they know which one is missing. Repeat this, with the children taking a turn to remove an item to identify the missing one.
  • Next time, explain you are going to make the game a little trickier by “secretly” removing one item for them to “work out” which one is missing (avoid the word “guess”), by looking at those that are still there. Cover the tray, secretly remove and hide one item and then uncover the tray for them to see and discuss.

What to try next

  • To begin with, you might introduce the game with fewer objects, until they are successful and are keen to make it trickier by increasing the amount.
  • Children take turns to be the “hider”. Be ready for the ‘joke’ when they remove every one! But, can we name them all?
  • Ask the children to suggest items to include on the tray.
  • Try a tray of similar items, e.g. all utensils or all blocks of different shapes. Does this make it trickier? It will depend on the familiarity of and interest in the items. Try 5 different dinosaurs!
  • Muddle up the objects before uncovering the tray. Does this make a difference to identifying what’s missing?
  • Play “What’s missing?” with arrangements of counters (subitising) ADD DIAGRAM?, or play “What’s changed?” with a repeating or simple pattern made by the child: “Hide your eyes and I’ll change your pattern – can you spot what I have done?”.

What to say

  • Open your eyes and look at the tray …
  • What do you think is missing? Why do you think that?
  • Are you sure? How do you know?
  • What was in that space, do we think?

What might happen

  • If a child is unable to identify the missing object, reveal it, and let them work with you, hiding (and naming quietly) an object for other children to identity.
  • Children might wildly name objects without looking at the tray. If so, using fewer objects and reminding them to look to see what is missing, pointing to the space, might help.

What matters

  • “What’s missing?” and “how do we know?” are fruitful mathematical questions that can – and should - be asked in many day-to-day situations where there is a structure or pattern to identify, e.g. laying the table, laying out a correct amount of items, tidying up, and so on. Asking “how do we know?” is an essential part of the activity, where we draw children’s attention to how they think and reason. Whilst we might not always receive an answer, it is an important question to ask. It can be followed, in time, by “Convince me you are right!”.1
  • It is important we use language like “think” and “work out”, and not “guess”, to draw children’s attention to the fact this is something they can logically puzzle out.

What to discuss

How are the children responding to our questions: “What’s missing?” and “how do we know?”

The Research

Did you know that…

…This activity involves short term memory and reasoning? It is aimed at developing children’s confidence in the skill of visualisation - imagining and manipulating spatial information in the mind’s eye. Visualisation is an important element of spatial reasoning.
